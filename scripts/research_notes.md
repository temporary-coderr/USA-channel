## Spotlight Effect — current episode

- **Claim in narration:** People tend to overestimate how much other people notice their appearance and behavior.
- **Primary source:** Gilovich, T., Medvec, V. H., & Savitsky, K. (2000). *The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance.* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222.
- **Publication details / DOI:** February 2000. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211
- **Authoritative abstract:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/
- **Corroborating Cornell University account:** https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/75e8b1b9-7949-4f1f-a74e-805025795976/content
- **What was studied:** Across five studies, the researchers examined how people estimate the extent to which their appearance and actions are noticed by others. In the T-shirt studies, participants wearing a potentially embarrassing or flattering shirt overestimated how many observers could recall the image.
- **Numerical wording:** The familiar Barry Manilow example is commonly summarized as wearers estimating roughly half the observers would identify the image, while about 23% actually did. Say “identify the person pictured,” not simply “notice the shirt”; the measure was recall/identification of the image.
- **Limitations / caveat:** This does not prove that nobody notices an awkward moment, that people forget it within a fixed number of seconds, or that nobody judges anyone. The study supports an average tendency to overestimate how much one's appearance or behavior stands out in those experimental situations.
- **Script wording:** Use “about 48% estimated” and “about 23% actually identified the person pictured.” Avoid “This is proven” and avoid claiming everyone forgets in ten seconds.
